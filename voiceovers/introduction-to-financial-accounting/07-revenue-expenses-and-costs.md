# Voice-over script: Revenue, Expenses & Costs

Lesson page: `revenue-expenses-and-costs.html`  
Length: about 2:00. Record one take per slide, matching the slides on the lesson page.

## Slide 1 · Hook (0:00–0:10)

You buy stock worth eighty thousand rupees for your shop. Money has gone out. So, is that an expense? Most students say yes. Let's see why the answer is: not yet.

## Slide 2 · Revenue (0:10–0:35)

Start with revenue. It's the income a business earns from its normal operations: selling goods or services. Other sources include interest, royalties and dividends received. Revenue increases equity. But money the owner puts in is not revenue. And revenue is recognised when the goods are delivered or the service is done, and payment is probable, not when the cash arrives.

## Slide 3 · Expenses (0:35–0:58)

Expenses are costs used up to earn that revenue. Operating expenses, like selling and administrative costs. Cost of goods sold, which is the cost of the stock you actually sold. Non-operating expenses, like losses on investments. Depreciation and amortisation, which spread an asset's cost over its life. And interest on borrowed money.

## Slide 4 · Classifying costs (0:58–1:25)

Costs can be classified in different ways. Direct costs can be traced to one product, like raw materials. Indirect costs can't, like electricity for the whole office. Fixed costs, like rent, stay the same within a normal range of output. Variable costs, like raw materials, rise and fall with output. And opportunity cost is the benefit you give up by choosing one option over another. It helps decisions, but it's never recorded in the books.

## Slide 5 · Example (1:25–1:48)

Now our stock. You buy goods for eighty thousand. That's a cost, held as an asset. You sell three quarters of them for ninety thousand. Only the cost of what you sold, sixty thousand, becomes an expense: cost of goods sold. Gross profit is thirty thousand. The other twenty thousand is still stock, an asset.

## Slide 6 · Recap (1:48–2:00)

So, is buying stock an expense? Not yet. It's a cost. It becomes an expense only when the goods are sold. Remember: revenue comes in from operations, expenses are costs used up to earn it, and profit is revenue minus expenses.
