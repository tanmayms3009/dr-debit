# Voice-over script: The Balance Sheet

Lesson page: `balance-sheet.html`  
Length: about 1:42. Record one take per slide, matching the slides on the lesson page.

## Slide 1 · Hook (0:00–0:10)

Two shops each made eighty thousand rupees of profit this year. One can pay all its bills next month. The other can't. Same profit, very different health. So how would you tell them apart?

## Slide 2 · What it is (0:10–0:25)

The balance sheet is a snapshot of the business on one date: what it owns, what it owes, and the owner's capital. It's dated as at thirty-first March, not for the year. And it always balances: assets equal liabilities plus capital.

## Slide 3 · Horizontal format (0:25–0:45)

In the traditional format for sole traders and partnerships, there are two sides. On the left: capital, long-term loans, and current liabilities like creditors. On the right: non-current assets like machinery, and current assets like stock, debtors and cash. Both sides total the same.

## Slide 4 · What it tells you (0:45–1:05)

What does it tell you? Liquidity: compare current assets with current liabilities to see if short-term debts can be paid. Creditworthiness: lenders check how risky it is to lend. And progress: compare balance sheets over several years to see how the business has changed.

## Slide 5 · Example (1:05–1:30)

Here's a shop's balance sheet. Capital, after adding profit and taking off drawings, four lakh fifty thousand. A loan of one lakh fifty thousand. Current liabilities, eighty thousand. Total, six lakh eighty thousand. On the other side, fixed assets of three lakh sixty thousand and current assets of three lakh twenty thousand. Also six lakh eighty thousand. Balanced.

## Slide 6 · Recap (1:30–1:42)

Back to our two shops. Profit doesn't tell you if bills can be paid. The balance sheet does. Compare current assets with current liabilities. The shop with more current assets than debts can pay. The other is in trouble.
