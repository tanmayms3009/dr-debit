# Voice-over script: GAAP & the Basic Concepts

Lesson page: `gaap-and-basic-concepts.html`  
Length: about 1:47. Record one take per slide, matching the slides on the lesson page.

## Slide 1 · Hook (0:00–0:10)

A business bought a building for fifty lakh rupees. Today it could sell for eighty lakh. So which number goes in the books? Fifty or eighty? The answer comes from a set of rules every accountant follows.

## Slide 2 · GAAP (0:10–0:28)

That's because of GAAP, generally accepted accounting principles. They're the common rules that make financial statements uniform, reliable and comparable. In India, they're set out in Accounting Standards and Ind AS. GAAP has two parts: concepts, the basic assumptions, and conventions, the accepted practices.

## Slide 3 · Who and what (0:28–0:50)

Two basic concepts. Business entity: the business is a separate accounting entity from its owner. So when the owner takes cash for personal use, it's recorded as drawings, not a business expense. Money measurement: only things that can be measured in money are recorded. A loyal customer base or a talented team isn't recorded at all.

## Slide 4 · When and how long (0:50–1:12)

Two concepts about time. Accounting period: we measure results for fixed periods, usually a year. In India, that's the financial year from first April to thirty-first March. Going concern: we assume the business will keep running. So assets are shown at cost less depreciation, not at what they'd fetch in a closing-down sale.

## Slide 5 · Example (1:12–1:35)

Now the cost concept. A machine's price is nine lakh fifty thousand. Transport costs thirty thousand and installation twenty thousand. So it's recorded at its full cost: ten lakh. It will last ten years, so depreciation is one lakh a year. After three years, it's shown at seven lakh, whatever its market price is.

## Slide 6 · Recap (1:35–1:47)

So, the building? It stays in the books at its cost of fifty lakh, less depreciation, not at the eighty lakh market value. That's the cost concept, backed by going concern. The only exception is if the business formally revalues it.
