Notes

Cash in and cash out

A cash flow statement tracks the inflow and outflow of cash in an organisation over a period. It is one of the main financial statements, alongside the income statement and balance sheet, and is especially useful for short-term financial planning and cash forecasting. Without enough cash, a business may have to borrow just to keep running.

Operating activities

Cash flows from the main business operations.

Cash received from customers; cash paid to suppliers, employees and for operating expenses.

Investing activities

Cash flows from buying and selling long-term assets and investments.

Purchase or sale of machinery and equipment; buying shares of other companies. For non-financial companies, interest and dividends received usually go here too.

Financing activities

Cash flows with owners and lenders.

Borrowing and repaying loans; issuing or buying back shares; paying dividends (and, for non-financial companies, interest paid). For sole traders: capital introduced and drawings.

Why it matters

Shows the cash side
Inventory and equipment purchases appear in the balance sheet; this statement shows the cash actually paid for them, and loan principal repaid
Right cash balance
Shows whether cash is idle, excessive or too low, so surplus can be invested or a shortage planned for
Focus on generating cash
Points to ways of improving cash, such as collecting receivables faster or negotiating better prices
Short-term planning
Helps anticipate cash shortfalls and arrange credit from banks in time

Common mistakes

Where marks are lost

WrongProfit and cash are the same.

RightCredit sales, asset purchases and loan repayments make them differ.

WrongDepreciation is a cash outflow.

RightIt reduces profit but no cash is paid.

WrongBuying machinery is an operating activity.

RightIt is a long-term asset, so it is an investing activity.

WrongTaking a loan is an operating inflow.

RightLoans taken or repaid are financing activities.
